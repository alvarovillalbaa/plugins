# Macro Analysis Reference

Reference for macro context relevant to startup and company decisions: indicators, rates, inflation, currency, and market-regime framing. Macro sets the backdrop — translate it into concrete implications for runway, fundraising, hiring, and pricing. Flag all forward-looking statements as scenarios, not facts.

## Why macro matters to an operating company

Macro rarely changes what a product *is*, but it changes the **cost of capital, customer budgets, and the fundraising window**. The job here is translation: "rates are higher for longer" → "valuations compress, raise takes longer, extend runway."

## Core indicators

| Indicator | What it signals | Operating implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GDP growth** | Aggregate demand | Customer budget expansion/contraction |
| **CPI / PCE inflation** | Price pressure | Input costs, wage demands, pricing power |
| **Policy rate (Fed funds / central bank)** | Cost of money | Discount rates, debt cost, valuation multiples |
| **Yield curve (2s10s)** | Growth/recession expectations | Inverted = recession signal; funding caution |
| **Unemployment / payrolls** | Labor slack | Hiring cost & ease, consumer demand |
| **PMI (mfg/services)** | Forward activity (>50 expansion) | Leading demand signal |
| **Consumer confidence / retail sales** | Spending appetite | B2C demand |
| **Credit spreads (IG/HY)** | Risk appetite | Capital availability for risky assets |
| **VIX** | Equity volatility / fear | Risk-on vs. risk-off regime |

Lead vs. lag: PMIs, yield curve, jobless claims, and new orders **lead**; GDP, unemployment rate, and earnings **lag**. Weight leading indicators for forward decisions.

## Interest rates

The single most important macro variable for venture-stage companies.

- **Mechanism**: higher risk-free rate → higher discount rate → future cash flows worth less today → multiple compression, hardest on long-duration (unprofitable, growth) assets.
- **Real vs. nominal**: real rate = nominal − expected inflation. Real rates drive valuation more than headline rates.
- **Curve shape**: steepening (long > short) often signals recovery/growth; inversion (short > long) signals expected cuts/recession.
- **Operating reads**:
  - High/rising rates → conserve cash, lengthen runway, expect down/flat rounds, prefer revenue over burn-funded growth.
  - Falling rates → fundraising window widens, multiples expand, growth gets rewarded again.

## Inflation

- **Demand-pull** (too much money chasing goods) vs. **cost-push** (supply shocks) — different durations and policy responses.
- **Effects on a company**:
  - Input/wage costs rise → margin pressure unless you have pricing power.
  - Customers tighten budgets → longer sales cycles, more ROI scrutiny.
  - Pricing power test: can you pass through cost increases without churn? Gross-margin stability through an inflationary period is a moat signal.
- **Watch**: core (ex food/energy) for trend, breakevens (TIPS) for expectations, wage growth for stickiness.

## Currency / FX

Relevant for companies with cross-border revenue, costs, or cash.
- Strong home currency → foreign revenue worth less when repatriated; cheaper imports.
- Weak home currency → exports/foreign revenue more valuable; imported inputs costlier.
- **Action**: natural hedging (match revenue and cost currencies), watch FX exposure on multi-currency runway, note translation effects on reported growth.

## Market regimes

Frame the environment, then set defaults. A regime is a persistent combination of growth + inflation + liquidity.

| Regime | Conditions | Posture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Risk-on / easy money** | Falling rates, ample liquidity, growth | Fundraise aggressively, invest in growth, multiples high |
| **Risk-off / tightening** | Rising rates, draining liquidity | Extend runway, prioritize efficiency, defensive |
| **Stagflation** | High inflation + weak growth | Protect margins, pricing power critical, cautious |
| **Goldilocks** | Steady growth, low inflation | Balanced growth + efficiency |
| **Recession/recovery** | Contraction then rebound | Survive the trough; be ready to invest into recovery |

## Translating macro → company decisions

For each decision, ask "what does the macro backdrop change?"
- **Runway**: tighter capital → target 24–36 months, not 12–18.
- **Fundraising timing**: raise into strength and open windows; don't wait for a "perfect" round in a closing market.
- **Hiring**: labor cost and availability shift with the cycle; tighter markets favor leaner teams.
- **Pricing**: inflationary periods are cover for price increases if you have value justification.
- **Sales motion**: budget scrutiny rises in downturns → lead with ROI/payback, shorten time-to-value.
- **Burn discipline**: in risk-off regimes, the burn multiple matters more than growth rate.

## Scenario framing

Never give a single macro forecast. Build at least three:
- **Base** (consensus / most likely) — plan against this.
- **Bull** (rates fall, demand recovers) — what upside requires.
- **Bear** (rates higher for longer, recession) — what survival requires.

For each, state the trigger indicators to watch and the pre-committed action if that scenario materializes. This turns macro from commentary into a decision tool.

## Sources & cautions

- Prefer primary data (central bank releases, BLS/BEA, PMI prints) over punditry.
- Markets price expectations, not current data — surprises move things, not levels.
- Macro forecasting has a poor track record; use it to size risk and prepare responses, not to predict precisely. Label every forward statement as a scenario.
