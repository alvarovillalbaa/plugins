# LinkedIn Article Example: "Why We Stopped Using Sprints After Year 2"

**Type**: Founder lesson / contrarian take  
**Length**: ~1,200 words  
**Audience**: Engineering leaders, startup founders  
**CTA**: Newsletter signup

---

## Hook (opening 3 lines — must stand alone in the feed)

We ran sprints for 18 months. Two-week cycles, standups, retrospectives — the full ritual.

Then we stopped. Here's what actually happened to our velocity.

---

## Section 1: The Setup (Context)

In 2022, we were a 6-person engineering team building a developer tool. We had just read the playbook — shape up, story points, velocity charts. We implemented it all.

By month 6, we had beautiful dashboards. We also had engineers who dreaded Mondays.

The problem wasn't the sprint length. It was that **our work didn't fit the format**.

---

## Section 2: The Turn (Insight)

We were building a product where the hardest problems weren't scope problems — they were discovery problems. We didn't know what "done" looked like until we were 40% through the work.

Sprints optimized for predictability. Our work optimized for exploration.

Three things kept breaking:
1. **Scope creep wasn't scope creep.** It was learning. We were punishing engineers for getting smarter mid-cycle.
2. **Two-week ceremonies for three-day problems.** Planning a sprint to build a feature that takes 2 days is overhead theater.
3. **Velocity as a metric incentivized the wrong behavior.** Engineers optimized for story points, not user impact.

---

## Section 3: What We Did Instead

We moved to a system we call "appetite-based development." Borrowed heavily from Basecamp's Shape Up, but simplified.

**Our new rules:**
- Work is sized by appetite (how much time is this worth?), not estimate (how long will this take?)
- Teams have 6-week cycles with a 2-week cooldown
- Cooldown is sacred — no new features, only fixing, cleaning, learning
- No sprints within cycles. Engineers own the full problem start to finish.

---

## Section 4: The Results

After 6 months on the new system:
- Fewer meetings (standups dropped from 5/week to 1)
- Engineers reported higher ownership and less anxiety
- We shipped faster — not because velocity went up, but because we stopped shipping the wrong things

One engineer told me: "I finally feel like I'm solving problems, not burning down a backlog."

---

## Closing + CTA

I'm not saying sprints are wrong for everyone. Large teams with stable product domains might find them essential.

But if you're a small team with high exploration work — building something genuinely new — the sprint ritual might be costing you more than it gives.

**What's worked for your team? I'm writing more about this in my newsletter — link in the comments.**
